Use a combination of signals rather than a single sender domain.

For a candidate search, try:

`category:promotions older:180d`

Then inspect a page or two and note whether the results contain receipts, shipping notices, invoices, password messages, or account alerts. For a particular newsletter sender, search:

`from:(news@example.com)`

For a marketing pattern that uses a recognizable subject phrase:

`from:(example.com) subject:(weekly OR digest OR newsletter)`

You can also search for bulk-mail headers with:

`{list-unsubscribe unsubscribe}`

That is useful for discovery, but not precise enough by itself for deletion. Messages from a mixed sender should generally be split by subject or keywords, for example:

`from:(updates.example.com) subject:(newsletter OR offers OR digest)`

while excluding terms that indicate operational mail:

`-subject:(receipt OR invoice OR shipping OR security OR password OR verification)`

Treat exclusions as a review aid, not a guarantee. Open representative messages and verify the sender, links, and purpose before applying a broad filter.

What is the safest way to handle the old backlog once these filters are working? I would like to delete obvious newsletters eventually, but I want a recovery path and a way to avoid deleting something just because it has an unsubscribe link.

[assistant]
Use a staged process with a quarantine label.

1. Keep the original messages intact while testing. For each trusted query, apply `Inbox Cleanup/Newsletter`; do not delete.
2. Review the label by sender and subject. Sort mentally into “definitely disposable,” “archive only,” and “uncertain.”
3. For definitely disposable mail, add a second label, such as `Inbox Cleanup/Delete After Review`.
4. Wait several days and search that label for protected terms:

`label:"Inbox Cleanup/Delete After Review" {receipt invoice order itinerary booking tax security verification password}`

Remove the delete label from anything that appears. Also check known senders for purchases, travel, financial services, and account access.
5. Delete only the remaining messages. Gmail moves them to Trash, normally for 30 days, giving you a recovery window.
6. After the retention period, empty Trash only if you are certain.

An unsubscribe link is evidence that a message may be bulk mail, not proof that it is disposable. Some important services include unsubscribe links in account notices. Use sender, subject, content, and your own history together. For the first deletion pass, limit it to a small, clearly identified sender or subject group and spot-check individual messages before selecting all.

I tried the searches and noticed some Gmail behavior I want to confirm. If I create a filter with “Skip the Inbox” and “Apply the label,” will it process existing messages, or only future mail? Also, if I archive messages, can I still find them with normal searches and keep them out of the inbox without marking them read?

[assistant]
When you create a Gmail filter, the “Also apply filter to matching conversations” option controls whether existing matching conversations are processed. If you leave it unchecked, the filter generally affects future incoming messages only. If you select it, Gmail applies the chosen actions to the current matching results as well; use that only after reviewing the result count and sample.

Archiving removes the `INBOX` label. It does not delete the message, and it remains searchable with queries such as:

`label:"Inbox Cleanup/Newsletter" newer:30d`

or simply:

`from:(digest@northstar.example)`

“Skip the Inbox” does not mark messages as read. Avoid selecting “Mark as read” if you want unread newsletters to remain visibly unread under the label. For existing mail, you can also select a reviewed batch manually, apply the label, and click Archive. That is often safer than checking the filter’s bulk-application option for a large, mixed result set.
