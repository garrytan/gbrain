I’m trying to work out a call time across three time zones: Pacific Time, Central European Time, and India Standard Time. Could you help me check the daylight-saving edge cases?

[assistant]
Sure. The main complication is that Pacific Time and Central European Time change clocks on different dates, while India Standard Time stays at UTC+5:30 all year.

For a normal date, the offsets are usually:

- Pacific Standard Time: UTC−8
- Pacific Daylight Time: UTC−7
- Central European Time: UTC+1
- Central European Summer Time: UTC+2
- India Standard Time: UTC+5:30

So the difference between Pacific and India is 13 hours 30 minutes during Pacific Standard Time, or 12 hours 30 minutes during Pacific Daylight Time. Central Europe is 4 hours 30 minutes ahead of Pacific during the winter combination, 9 hours 30 minutes ahead of Pacific during the summer combination, depending on which region is on daylight time, and so on. It is best to calculate from UTC for the specific date.

Let’s use a hypothetical call date of March 15. If the Pacific location is on daylight time, Europe is still on standard time, and India does not change, what would 9:00 AM Pacific be in the other two zones?

[assistant]
On March 15 under those assumptions:

- Pacific Daylight Time is UTC−7.
- Central European Time is UTC+1.
- India Standard Time is UTC+5:30.

9:00 AM Pacific Daylight Time converts to 4:00 PM UTC. Therefore:

- Central European Time: 5:00 PM
- India Standard Time: 9:30 PM

So the schedule would be:

```text
Pacific Daylight Time       09:00
Central European Time       17:00
India Standard Time         21:30
```

That assumes the date is the same local calendar date in all three places. In this example, it is.

Now compare that with a date when all three are in their standard or non-DST offsets. What would 9:00 AM Pacific be then?

[assistant]
If all three locations are on the offsets you listed as standard or non-DST:

- Pacific Standard Time: UTC−8
- Central European Time: UTC+1
- India Standard Time: UTC+5:30

Then 9:00 AM Pacific Standard Time is 5:00 PM UTC.

The corresponding local times are:

```text
Pacific Standard Time       09:00
Central European Time       18:00
India Standard Time         22:30
```

Compared with the March 15 example, Europe is one hour later and India is three hours later relative to the Pacific clock, because Pacific has moved from UTC−8 to UTC−7 while Europe has not yet moved from UTC+1 to UTC+2.

Yes. India remains UTC+5:30. Using 9:00 AM Pacific as the starting point:

Pacific status   Europe status   Pacific UTC   UTC time   Europe time   India time
Standard         Standard        UTC−8         17:00      18:00         22:30

The key point is that India’s time depends only on the Pacific offset in this table, while Europe’s time depends on both the Pacific and European offsets.

For the European column, “standard” means UTC+1 and “daylight” means UTC+2. For the Pacific column, “standard” means UTC−8 and “daylight” means UTC−7.

Could we check the actual transition windows? I’m especially concerned about the spring dates when one region has changed clocks and another has not.

[assistant]
Yes. The exact dates vary by year, but the general pattern is:

- United States and Canada generally change to daylight time on the second Sunday in March and return on the first Sunday in November.
- Most European countries generally change on the last Sunday in March and return on the last Sunday in October.
- India does not use seasonal clock changes.

That creates a period in March when Pacific is already on daylight time but Central Europe is still on standard time. It also creates a shorter period in October or early November when Europe may have returned to standard time while Pacific remains on daylight time.

For a reliable check, use a named year and a time-zone-aware tool rather than assuming the transition dates. For example, a system with the IANA zones can show the offsets:

```text
America/Los_Angeles
Europe/Paris
Asia/Kolkata
```

The IANA names matter because “PST,” “CET,” and similar abbreviations can be ambiguous or can hide the seasonal change.

2027-03-14    09:00 PDT         16:00      17:00 CET      21:30 IST
2027-03-28    09:00 PDT         16:00      18:00 CEST     21:30 IST

The reason March 14 differs from March 28 is that the United States changes clocks earlier in March, while Paris remains on standard time until the last Sunday of March. By November 7, both regions have completed their autumn changes, so the standard offsets apply.

The date shown is the local date in Los Angeles. In these examples, the converted times remain on the same calendar date in Paris and Kolkata.

Can you verify those results with command-line output, including the UTC offsets rather than just abbreviations?

[assistant]
Yes. On a system with GNU `date` and a time-zone database containing the 2027 rules, you can run:

```sh
for d in 2027-03-14 2027-03-28 2027-11-07; do
  TZ=America/Los_Angeles date -d "$d 09:00" \
    '+%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S %Z %z'
  TZ=Europe/Paris date -d "$(TZ=America/Los_Angeles date -d "$d 09:00" '+%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S %z')" \
    '+%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S %Z %z'
done
```

A clearer approach is to first convert the Pacific time to UTC and then format that instant in each zone. Representative output is:

```text
2027-03-14 09:00:00 PDT -0700
2027-03-14 17:00:00 CET +0100

2027-03-28 09:00:00 PDT -0700
2027-03-28 18:00:00 CEST +0200

2027-11-07 09:00:00 PST -0800
2027-11-07 18:00:00 CET +0100
```

For Kolkata, use the same UTC instant:

```text
2027-03-14 21:30:00 IST +0530
2027-03-28 21:30:00 IST +0530
2027-11-07 22:30:00 IST +0530
```

The exact abbreviation text can vary by operating system, but the numeric offsets are the important part.
