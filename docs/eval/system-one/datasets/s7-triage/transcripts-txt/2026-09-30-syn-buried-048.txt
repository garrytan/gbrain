[assistant]
Use one of these methods:

1. Push a clean finger into the soil about one inch, roughly to the first knuckle. Check in two or three spots near the edge of the pot, avoiding the plant’s central stems. If the soil feels cool and damp, wait. If it feels dry and crumbly, water.
2. Insert an untreated wooden chopstick or skewer about two inches deep. Leave it for a few seconds, then pull it out. Dark, damp soil clinging to it means the plant is not ready; a mostly clean, dry stick means it probably is.
3. Lift the pot after watering and remember its weight. When it feels noticeably lighter, combine that clue with a finger check.

Because the leaves block the surface, check near the rim. Do not rely on leaf drooping alone; Calatheas can droop from dry soil, heat, or low humidity.

Back to the plant: my apartment is fairly dry, especially in winter. Should I water more often to compensate for low humidity?

[assistant]
Usually, no. Low humidity increases water loss through the leaves, but watering more often can leave the roots wet and create root-rot risk. Treat soil moisture and air humidity as separate issues.

Keep the twice-weekly soil checks, and adjust watering only when the soil reaches the top-inch-dry trigger. For humidity, you can:

- Group plants together, leaving enough space for airflow.
- Use a room humidifier and clean it regularly.
- Keep the plant away from heating vents, radiators, and cold drafts.
- Place it in bright, indirect light rather than intense direct sun.

A pebble tray may provide a small local effect, but the pot must sit above the water on pebbles, never directly in standing water. Brown leaf edges can indicate dry air, inconsistent moisture, salt buildup, or too much sun, so they are not by themselves a reason to water more.

Do not water yet if the lower root zone is still wet. The top inch can dry sooner because it is exposed to air, while the lower soil remains saturated. For an 8-inch pot, check both the surface zone and deeper soil near the drainage holes.

- Top inch dry and deeper soil slightly moist: wait two or three days, then recheck.

Moisture meters are useful for trends but are not always accurate in dense or root-filled soil. Confirm their reading with a finger or wooden skewer, and avoid repeatedly probing directly through the plant’s main root area.

Could you explain what “evenly moist” should feel like in practical terms? I don’t want to interpret it as “wet all the time.”

[assistant]
“Evenly moist” means the soil has some retained moisture throughout the root zone, but it is not waterlogged. In practical terms, after watering, the mix should feel uniformly damp rather than muddy. Between waterings, the upper inch should become dry, while soil below it may still feel slightly cool or moist.

Useful distinctions:

- Damp: soil feels cool and holds together lightly, but no water comes out when gently squeezed.
- Wet or waterlogged: soil is saturated, muddy, shiny, or stays heavy and wet for many days.
- Dry: soil is pale, loose, dusty, and may pull away from the pot edge.

For your plant, aim for a cycle of thorough watering followed by gradual drying in the upper layer. Do not keep the entire pot constantly wet to satisfy the phrase “evenly moist.”
