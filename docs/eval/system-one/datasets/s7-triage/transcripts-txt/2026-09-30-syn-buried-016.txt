Why is the sky blue?

[assistant]
The sky looks blue because of Rayleigh scattering. Sunlight appears white, but it contains many colors, each with a different wavelength. As sunlight passes through Earth’s atmosphere, tiny nitrogen and oxygen molecules scatter the shorter blue and violet wavelengths much more strongly than the longer red and orange wavelengths.

We see blue rather than violet partly because our eyes are more sensitive to blue, and because some violet light is absorbed in the upper atmosphere. The scattered blue light reaches your eyes from all directions, making the daytime sky appear blue.

[user]
If violet scatters even more strongly, why doesn’t the sky look violet?

[assistant]
There are three main reasons. First, sunlight contains somewhat less violet than blue light. Second, human eyes detect blue more efficiently than violet; our visual system’s short-wavelength cones respond to both, but blue contributes more strongly to the color we perceive. Third, some of the shortest-wavelength light is absorbed in the upper atmosphere.

So the sky’s actual scattered light includes violet, blue, and some nearby wavelengths, but the combined signal is interpreted by human vision as blue or blue-cyan.

Does the sky have the same color everywhere on Earth?

[assistant]
No. It varies with location, altitude, weather, pollution, and the Sun’s position.

At high altitude, there is less atmosphere above you, so the sky can look a deeper, darker blue. Humidity and aerosols can make it appear pale or whitish because larger particles scatter many visible wavelengths more evenly. Near sunrise and sunset, sunlight travels through a longer atmospheric path. More blue and green light is scattered out of the direct beam, leaving warmer reds, oranges, and pinks.

The sky also looks different away from the Sun than it does near the Sun, where scattered light and aerosols can produce a bright whitish region.

[user]
Why is the sky red at sunset if the atmosphere itself hasn’t changed color?

[assistant]
The atmosphere has not turned red; the viewing geometry has changed. When the Sun is low, its light passes through a much longer path in the atmosphere. Rayleigh scattering removes proportionally more blue and violet light from the direct sunlight before it reaches you. The remaining direct beam is enriched in longer wavelengths, especially red and orange.

Dust, smoke, and larger aerosol particles can enhance or soften the effect. They may scatter and absorb light in ways that produce vivid red sunsets, but they can also make the sunset brownish or hazy.

Can you explain the difference between Rayleigh scattering and Mie scattering?

[assistant]
Rayleigh scattering occurs when particles are much smaller than the wavelength of light, such as individual gas molecules. Its strength rises sharply toward shorter wavelengths, approximately proportional to 1 divided by wavelength to the fourth power. That is why molecular scattering separates blue light from red light so effectively.

Mie scattering occurs when particles are similar in size to, or larger than, the wavelength, such as cloud droplets, dust, smoke particles, and pollen. It has a weaker wavelength dependence and often scatters several visible colors more evenly. Clouds therefore look white or gray rather than blue: their droplets scatter the colors together.

Why do clouds look gray underneath?

[assistant]
A thick cloud can scatter sunlight many times before the light exits. The top and edges receive direct sunlight and may look bright white. The lower part receives less light because the cloud droplets scatter and absorb some sunlight on the way down, and because the cloud can block light from the blue sky above.

The result is less light reaching your eyes from the underside. Your visual system interprets that lower brightness as gray, while very thick storm clouds can look nearly charcoal.

Are rainbows caused by scattering too?

[assistant]
Rainbows are mainly caused by refraction, internal reflection, and dispersion in water droplets rather than by atmospheric scattering.

As sunlight enters a raindrop, it bends because light travels at a different speed in water than in air. The different colors bend by slightly different amounts, which is dispersion. Light then reflects off the inside back surface of the droplet and bends again as it exits. A large number of droplets send different colors toward your eyes at particular angles, producing the arc.

[user]
Why is a rainbow an arc instead of a straight band?

[assistant]
The arc comes from the geometry of the light reaching your eye. For a primary rainbow, light is concentrated at an angle of about 42 degrees from the direction opposite the Sun, called the antisolar direction. Every raindrop positioned on a cone around that direction can send rainbow light toward you.

The ground usually hides the lower part of that cone, so from the ground you see an arc. From an airplane or a high mountain, you may sometimes see much more of the circle, provided there are droplets below your viewpoint and the Sun is low enough.

[user]
Why does the order of colors in the primary rainbow always stay the same?

[assistant]
In a primary rainbow, red is on the outside and violet is on the inside. The order comes from dispersion: water refracts violet light more strongly than red light. After entering, reflecting inside, and exiting the drop, the colors leave at slightly different angles.

For a typical primary rainbow, red light reaches the observer at roughly 42 degrees from the antisolar point, while violet is closer to 40 degrees. Since each color occupies its own angular range, their order remains consistent around the arc.

[user]
Can you see a rainbow when the Sun is high in the sky?

[assistant]
A primary rainbow can form whenever sunlight reaches raindrops opposite the Sun, but from the ground the Sun must generally be below about 42 degrees above the horizon for the main arc to rise above the horizon. The lower the Sun, the higher the rainbow appears.

That is why rainbows are common in the morning and late afternoon, especially when rain is falling in the part of the sky opposite the Sun. At noon in many locations, the Sun is too high for the primary arc to be visible from level ground, though an observer in an aircraft can have a different view.

[user]
What is a secondary rainbow?

[assistant]
A secondary rainbow is produced when light undergoes two internal reflections inside each raindrop instead of one. It appears outside the primary rainbow, at a larger angle—roughly 51 to 54 degrees from the antisolar direction.

Its colors are reversed: red is on the inside and violet on the outside. The second internal reflection sends less light toward the observer, so a secondary rainbow is usually fainter. The region between the two bows, called Alexander’s band, can look noticeably darker because of the way light is distributed around the rainbow angles.

Back to rainbows: what causes supernumerary rainbows?

[assistant]
Supernumerary rainbows are faint extra bands that appear just inside the primary bow, usually as alternating pastel pink, green, and violet stripes. They are caused by wave interference, not by separate additional reflections.

Light waves following slightly different paths through similarly sized water droplets can arrive at the observer either in phase, which reinforces a color, or out of phase, which weakens it. The effect is easiest to see when droplets are relatively uniform in size, because their interference patterns line up. Uneven droplet sizes blur the bands, while small droplets can make the colors broader and more diffuse.

Why can a rainbow seem to move when I move?

[assistant]
A rainbow is not a fixed object located at a particular distance. It is a viewing effect created by sunlight, water droplets, and your position. When you move, the set of droplets whose outgoing light reaches your eyes at the required angles changes, so the rainbow appears to move with you.

Someone standing nearby sees a rainbow formed by a different collection of droplets. In that sense, each observer has a personal rainbow. You cannot walk toward the end of a rainbow and reach it, because the apparent location changes as your viewpoint changes.

Can moonlight make a rainbow?

[assistant]
Yes. A moonbow forms when bright moonlight passes through raindrops or mist under the right geometry. The optical process is essentially the same as for a solar rainbow: refraction, dispersion, internal reflection, and refraction again.

Because the Moon is much dimmer than the Sun, moonbows usually appear white or faintly colored to human eyes, especially under dark-adapted conditions. Long-exposure photographs can reveal stronger colors. A nearly full Moon, a dark sky, and rain or spray opposite the Moon provide favorable conditions.
