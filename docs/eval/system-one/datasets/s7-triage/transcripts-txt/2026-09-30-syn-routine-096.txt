Can you help me find a workable call time across Pacific Time, Central European Time, and India Standard Time? I’m trying to avoid daylight-saving surprises.

[assistant]
Sure. The main complication is that Pacific Time and Central European Time change clocks seasonally, while India Standard Time stays at UTC+5:30 all year.

For a reliable comparison, it helps to separate the year into periods:

- Pacific Standard Time (PST): UTC−8
- Pacific Daylight Time (PDT): UTC−7
- Central European Time (CET): UTC+1
- Central European Summer Time (CEST): UTC+2
- India Standard Time (IST): UTC+5:30

The exact transition dates differ between North America and Europe, so there are short windows when the usual offset changes.

| Pacific | Europe when CET | India |
|---|---:|---:|
| 10:00 PST | 19:00 CET | 23:30 IST |
| 10:30 PST | 19:30 CET | 00:00 IST next day |
| 11:00 PST | 20:00 CET | 00:30 IST next day |
| 10:00 PDT | 18:00 CET | 22:30 IST |
| 10:30 PDT | 18:30 CET | 23:00 IST |

When Europe is on summer time, subtract one hour from the European entries in the table. India’s time does not change.

That means 10:00 Pacific is the cleanest general compromise, but it can land at 22:30, 23:30, or 00:30 in India depending on the season.

[assistant]
09:30 Pacific has four common outcomes because the two seasonal clocks do not always change on the same dates:

- PST + CET: 18:30 Europe, 23:00 India
- PST + CEST: 19:30 Europe, 23:00 India
- PDT + CET: 17:30 Europe, 22:00 India
- PDT + CEST: 18:30 Europe, 22:00 India

So it is always before midnight in India. The European time is either 17:30, 18:30, or 19:30, depending on the season and the transition gap.

The key point is that India is 13 hours 30 minutes ahead of PST and 12 hours 30 minutes ahead of PDT. Whether Europe is on CET or CEST affects only Europe’s displayed time.

For 2026, the scheduled clock changes are:

- United States and Canada: Sunday, March 8, 2026, at 02:00 local standard time; clocks move forward to 03:00.

- CET is UTC+1, so 09:30 PST becomes 18:30 CET, and 09:30 PDT becomes 17:30 CET.

Can you give me a concise operational checklist for setting this up, including how to inspect the event after creation?

[assistant]
Use a named time zone rather than a fixed offset:

1. Set the event’s primary time zone to `America/Los_Angeles`.
2. Enter the local start time as `09:00`.
3. Make it a weekday occurrence only if needed; avoid relying on a manually typed UTC offset.
4. Check the displayed time in a Central European zone and an India zone.
5. Inspect occurrences on dates immediately after:
   - March 8, 2026
   - March 29, 2026
   - October 25, 2026
   - November 1, 2026
6. Confirm that the European display is 17:00, 18:00, or 19:00 as expected, and India is 21:30 or 22:30.
7. If the application supports exporting the event, inspect the calendar data for a zone identifier rather than only `UTC−7` or `UTC−8`.
8. Recheck after importing into any other calendar application, because some tools preserve the wall-clock time while others preserve a fixed offset.

For a one-off event, you can also convert via UTC:

- `09:00 PDT` = `16:00 UTC`
- `09:00 PST` = `17:00 UTC`

But for a recurring event, use `America/Los_Angeles` so the Pacific daylight-saving changes are applied automatically.

What would an iCalendar snippet look like for a weekly event at 09:00 Pacific, and what should I look for in the output?

[assistant]
A recurring iCalendar event can use a `VTIMEZONE` component plus a local `DTSTART`. A shortened illustrative structure is:

BEGIN:VEVENT
UID:weekly-0900-example
DTSTAMP:20260101T120000Z
DTSTART;TZID=America/Los_Angeles:20260105T090000
RRULE:FREQ=WEEKLY;BYDAY=MO,TU,WE,TH,FR
SUMMARY:Routine time-zone check
END:VEVENT

In a complete `.ics` file, the calendar may include a `VTIMEZONE` definition for `America/Los_Angeles`, or the receiving application may resolve the zone from its time-zone database.

Look for these details:

- `TZID=America/Los_Angeles`, not just `TZID=PST` or `TZID=UTC-8`.
- A local `DTSTART` value of `20260105T090000`.
- A weekly recurrence rule, if you want weekdays.
- No `Z` suffix on the local `DTSTART`; `Z` would indicate UTC.
- A valid `UID` and `DTSTAMP`.
- The imported occurrences staying at 09:00 Pacific after March 8 and November 1.

If the output instead contains something like `DTSTART:20260105T170000Z` with a fixed UTC recurrence, inspect later occurrences carefully. That may preserve 17:00 UTC rather than 09:00 Pacific, causing the displayed Pacific time to shift when daylight saving changes.

Could you provide a shell-based sanity check that converts those sample timestamps, and mention any limitations of the local time-zone database?

[assistant]
On systems with GNU `date`, you can set `TZ` for each conversion. For example:

```sh
for x in \
  "2026-03-02 09:00:00" \
  "2026-03-09 09:00:00" \
  "2026-03-30 09:00:00" \
  "2026-10-19 09:00:00" \
  "2026-10-26 09:00:00" \
  "2026-11-02 09:00:00"
do
  utc=$(TZ=America/Los_Angeles date -d "$x" -u '+%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S UTC')
  printf '%s Pacific -> %s\n' "$x" "$utc"
done
```

Typical output is:

```text
2026-03-02 09:00:00 Pacific -> 2026-03-02 17:00:00 UTC
2026-03-09 09:00:00 Pacific -> 2026-03-09 16:00:00 UTC
2026-03-30 09:00:00 Pacific -> 2026-03-30 16:00:00 UTC
2026-10-19 09:00:00 Pacific -> 2026-10-19 16:00:00 UTC
2026-10-26 09:00:00 Pacific -> 2026-10-26 16:00:00 UTC
2026-11-02 09:00:00 Pacific -> 2026-11-02 17:00:00 UTC
```

To display each UTC instant in the other zones, first generate an unambiguous UTC string and then run:

```sh
instant='2026-03-09 16:00:00 UTC'
TZ=Europe/Paris date -d "$instant" '+%Y-%m-%d %H:%M %Z'
TZ=Asia/Kolkata date -d "$instant" '+%Y-%m-%d %H:%M %Z'
```

Expected output for that example:

```text
2026-03-09 17:00 CET
2026-03-09 21:30 IST
```

Limitations:

- The commands depend on GNU `date`; BSD/macOS `date` uses different options.
- The installed tzdata package may be old or patched.
- `Europe/Paris` represents the Central European rules used in this example; a different European location may have different historical or legal rules.
- `Asia/Kolkata` is the IANA identifier commonly used for India Standard Time.
- Transition-day parsing can be implementation-dependent for nonexistent or repeated local times, although 09:00 is far from the usual transition hour.
- A calendar application may use its own time-zone data rather than the shell’s database, so compare both when troubleshooting.
